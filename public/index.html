How the math works exactly what goes into the numbers, click to expand

Dollars. You enter amounts in today's dollars. The engine then runs in actual future dollars: every cost and income rises at its own rate (or at inflation if you leave the rate blank), and returns are actual returns. Every result is converted back to today's dollars, so $1M at age 80 means what $1M buys today.

Each year, in order: (1) money in the bucket plan is split across the three buckets and earns the blended return, while any account in its own fund earns that fund's return, (2) contributions are added, (3) the year's cash flow lands. Leftover income is saved to the taxable account; a shortfall is withdrawn from the accounts.

Own-fund accounts (like a 401(k) in a target-date fund) aren't part of the split. They're treated as long-term money, so they reduce how much the bucket plan needs to hold in equities. Contributions go in at year end for the number of years you enter on each account.

Cash flow each year = income + Social Security + property cash flow + sale proceeds − spending − healthcare. Social Security and healthcare rise with inflation. Mortgage payments are fixed dollar amounts and don't rise.

The split. Every future withdrawal is assigned to a bucket by how far away it is: years 1 through your capital preservation cutoff go to capital preservation, the next years through your high income cutoff go to high income, and the rest go to equities. Each withdrawal is valued at what it costs today, following the path its money takes (it waits in equities, then moves to high income, then to capital preservation). The split always keeps those proportions: if you have more than the plan needs, every bucket gets the same extra share; if you have less, every bucket is short by the same share and the shortfall shows up as the gap. So as the years left get fewer, more of the money sits in capital preservation. The split is redone every year.

Withdrawals and tax. Money comes out of the taxable account first, then traditional IRA / 401(k), then Roth; within each type, bucket-plan money is used before own-fund money. Enough is taken to cover tax, so the after-tax amount pays the bills:

  • Taxable: capital-gains rate × the gain share of what's sold. Gain share = (value − cost basis) ÷ value. Growth raises the value but not the basis, so the taxed share rises over time. A sale lowers the basis in proportion (average cost).
  • Traditional IRA / 401(k): ordinary income rate on the whole amount, plus a 10% penalty before age 59½.
  • Roth: tax-free. (Withdrawals before 59½ are flagged but not penalized.)

Do I have enough? The plan runs once with what you have. If it reaches your goal (spend down to zero, leave an amount, or never drop below a floor) without the balance going negative, you have enough. If not, the gap is the smallest extra amount, invested today as new money in a taxable account, that reaches the goal. It's found by trying amounts until one just works.

Stress tests rerun the same plan under bad cases: a rental vacancy plus a big repair plus a delayed sale, a market crash today, spending 20% higher, and all at once.

What it doesn't do (yet): no random market paths (every year earns the expected return; Monte Carlo is planned), no required minimum distributions (slightly optimistic), no tax brackets (one effective rate each), rebalancing is treated as tax-free, and every account in the bucket plan holds the same mix. Every number here follows from the inputs you can see.
