How the math works exactly what goes into the numbers, click to expand

Dollars. You enter amounts in today's dollars. The engine runs in actual future dollars: every cost and income rises at its own rate (or at inflation if you leave the rate blank). Every result is converted back to today's dollars, so $1M at age 80 means what $1M buys today.

Cash flow each year = income + Social Security − spending − healthcare. Social Security and healthcare rise with inflation. You and your spouse each live to the plan-to age; the plan runs until the younger of you reaches it, and the survivor keeps the larger Social Security check.

The split. Every future withdrawal is assigned to a bucket by how far away it is: the next years (through your capital preservation cutoff) to capital preservation, the following years (through your high income cutoff) to high income. Each is valued at what it costs today. The buckets fill in that order: capital preservation first, then high income, then everything else in equities. The same rule applies every year to the end of the plan; there is no separate late-life shift. An account dedicated to one bucket counts toward that bucket's target, and the bucket plan fills whatever is left.

Each year, in order: (1) every bucket earns its return (dedicated accounts move with their bucket), (2) contributions are added, (3) required minimum distributions come out of traditional IRAs and 401(k)s, then the year's cash flow lands. Leftover income (and RMD money the year doesn't need) is saved to the taxable account; a shortfall is withdrawn from accounts, and on the bucket side it comes out of capital preservation first, then high income. (4) Refilling: after a year equities rose, the bucket plan is reset to its targets; dedicated accounts are never moved. After a year equities fell, nothing is sold to refill; the plan keeps spending from the safe buckets and lets equities recover.

Withdrawals and tax. Money comes out of the taxable account first, then traditional IRA / 401(k), then Roth; within each type, dedicated capital preservation accounts first, then the bucket plan, then dedicated high income, then dedicated equities. Enough is taken to cover tax:

  • Taxable: capital-gains rate × the gain share of what's sold. Gain share = (value − cost basis) ÷ value. Growth raises the value but not the basis, so the taxed share rises over time.
  • Traditional IRA / 401(k): ordinary income rate on the whole amount, plus a 10% penalty before age 59½.
  • Roth: tax-free.

Required minimum distributions (RMDs). From the owner's RMD age (73 if born 1951–1959, 75 if born 1960 or later), each traditional IRA and 401(k) must pay out at least last year-end's balance ÷ the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table divisor for the owner's age (24.6 at 75, 20.2 at 80, 12.2 at 90). RMDs are taxed at the ordinary income rate and first pay for the year's spending; the rest is reinvested in the taxable account. When the owner dies, the survivor treats the account as their own, so RMDs follow the survivor's age. Roth IRAs have no RMDs.

Simulated futures (Monte Carlo). The plan is run through 1,000 futures. In each, every year's return for each bucket is drawn at random: the expected return you entered is what money compounds at in a typical future, and the swing sets how bumpy the years are. Equities and high income tend to move together; capital preservation moves on its own; a dedicated account moves exactly with its bucket. Inflation is fixed. The draws are repeatable, so the same inputs always give the same answer.

Chance of success is the share of futures that reach your goal (spend down to zero, leave an amount, or never drop below a floor) without running out. The gap is the smallest extra amount, invested today in the recommended split, that lifts the chance of success to your target. The 50% / 80% / 90% outcomes are balances that half, 80%, and 90% of futures end at least as well as.

The recommended split, the by-age table, and the year-by-year cash flow use the expected returns (every year exactly average), so they're easy to read.

What it doesn't do (yet): no tax brackets (one effective rate each, and Social Security is treated as tax-free), rebalancing is treated as tax-free, and inflation doesn't vary.
